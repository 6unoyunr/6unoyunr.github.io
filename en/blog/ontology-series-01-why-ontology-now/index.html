I Went to an AI Bootcamp and Aristotle Showed Up: Why Ontology Is Back

Junyoung Park · 2026-09-29 · 12 min

This is Part 1 of a three-part introduction to ontology. Company A, Product Z, and Company B are fictional examples.

I signed up for an ontology bootcamp. Aristotle showed up in the opening minutes.

I had come for AI, and suddenly we were talking about an ancient Greek philosopher. For a moment, I wondered if I had walked into the wrong room.

Then the instructor asked a surprisingly corporate question.

Who exactly counts as a “customer” at your company?

Only the person who pays? Does a prospect count? If one corporation has several subsidiaries, are they one customer or several? And when Sales says “customer,” does Finance mean the same thing?

If you have ever connected two enterprise systems, you can probably feel the trap closing already. Companies often have plenty of data. The trouble is that different teams use different names for the same thing—and the same name for different things.

That is the territory ontology deals with. It is not a spell that injects universal truth into an AI. It is a way to make an organization's concepts and relationships explicit enough for software to follow them.

Sounds simple. The sentence is the simple part.

Ontology: intimidating name, practical problem

Before it became a computer-science term, ontology belonged to philosophy. It asks what exists, which categories those things belong to, and how they relate to one another.

The word carries that question in its roots: the Greek on/ontos, meaning “being” or “that which exists,” and -logia, meaning study or account. Philosophers had been classifying existence since antiquity, but the word ontology itself arrived much later. Aristotle did not personally coin the English term. Let's clear his name while we are here.

Computer science borrowed the philosophical question and made it aggressively practical:

What exists in our business domain, and how are those things related?

For a manufacturer, the answer might include products, components, production lines, suppliers, contracts, and delivery dates. A product uses components. A supplier provides them. A delayed delivery affects the production plan and the shipping schedule. An ontology describes not only the nouns, but also their relationships and rules.

In 1993, Tom Gruber defined an ontology as “an explicit specification of a conceptualization.” Later work emphasized that the specification should also be formal and shared, leading to the widely used formulation “a formal, explicit specification of a shared conceptualization.”

That definition is technically compact and emotionally unhelpful, so let's unpack it.

  • Conceptualization means deciding which concepts we use to model reality.
  • Shared means several people agree to use those concepts in the same way; this is not a private note.
  • Explicit means writing down the knowledge hidden inside “everyone knows that.”
  • Formal means representing it in a form that software can query and reason over.

Suppose Company A's sales team calls every prospect a customer. Finance reserves the word for companies that have generated revenue. Support also calls an end user who filed a ticket a customer.

People can sometimes recover the intended meaning from context. Computers generally cannot. To be fair, people regularly fail at this too. There is a reason the meeting keeps running long.

Designing an ontology means refusing to hide all of those meanings behind one word. We distinguish prospects, contracted customers, paying customers, and end users, then describe where they overlap and where they do not. Put simply, an ontology is a machine-readable map of how an organization sees its world.

Isn't this just good database design?

That is the natural next question.

“Are we just saying we should design our schemas properly?”

Half right. A well-designed relational database already contains a great deal of domain knowledge. It has customer and contract tables, and foreign keys express relationships between them. Ontology does not suddenly make relational databases obsolete.

The problem is that a real company rarely has only one database.

Company B appears as VENDOR in the ERP system and as PARTNER in production management. Its legal name appears in contracts, and every system may assign it a different ID. Add meeting notes, email, and PDFs, and things become even more fun. “Fun” is doing some work in that sentence.

An ontology does not require throwing those systems away and rebuilding everything in a new store. The source RDBs can remain where they are. Their tables, columns, and documents can be mapped to a shared semantic layer that says which real-world concepts they represent. A system might then generate SQL through that model, or copy only the data that benefits from graph traversal into a graph store.

This is why ontologies often appear beside knowledge graphs. They are close relatives, but they are not synonyms.

Imagine Company A has connected its data like this:

Product Z ──uses──▶ Battery B-21
Company B ──supplies──▶ Battery B-21
Company B ──caused──▶ Delivery Delay D-7
Delivery Delay D-7 ──affected──▶ September Production Plan

That is the shape of a knowledge graph: concrete entities and facts connected by relationships. An ontology goes one level deeper. It defines statements such as “a battery is a kind of critical component,” “a delivery delay is a supply event,” and “a supplier must be a legal organization.”

If a graph is a map of connected facts, an ontology is the legend and grammar used to read that map. In production systems, they are often used together, so the boundary can get fuzzy. Still, drawing attractive nodes and edges does not automatically give you an ontology. A nice relationship diagram and a semantic model are not the same deliverable.

The Korean diagram contrasts a fact map on the left with the concepts and rules needed to interpret it on the right. “We connected the data” and “we made the meaning of those connections explicit” are two different claims.

Can't generative AI just read all of this?

Modern LLMs can read PDFs and tables, and they can write SQL. Put documents into a vector database, add RAG, and you have the beginnings of an internal chatbot. Do we really need something as elaborate as an ontology?

One point matters here:

Not every AI product needs an ontology.

If a chatbot only needs to find the number of vacation days in an HR policy, document retrieval and RAG may be enough. If the question is monthly revenue, one tested SQL query may be faster, cheaper, and more accurate. Building a grand semantic system for a small problem is an excellent way to make the infrastructure larger than the value it provides.

The situation changes when a question crosses systems and relationships.

Find the suppliers that delayed critical components for Product Z in the last six months. Check whether those delays were associated with lower shipment volume by country, and tell me whether we have alternative suppliers.

Answering that requires finding products and components in the production system, suppliers in procurement, and actual delivery dates in logistics. It also requires shipment figures from sales and alternative-sourcing terms from contracts. Before any of that works, the organization needs a consistent meaning for “critical component,” “delivery delay,” and “alternative supplier.”

RAG is good at retrieving relevant documents. But retrieving text with similar wording is not the same as identifying one entity across systems and connecting facts that satisfy precise conditions. A meeting note appearing in the search results does not prove a business cause.

The agent sounds very sure of itself. Its evidence is one sheet of paper. “I found a related document” and “I verified the evidence” are not interchangeable.

From AI that talks to AI that acts

Generative AI mainly produces answers. Agentic AI takes a goal, plans several steps, and uses tools.

Suppose Company A asks an agent: “Find the suppliers with high delivery risk and create a review request for Procurement.” The agent might query the production plan and delivery history, inspect alternative-sourcing clauses in contracts, identify the responsible buyer, and create a draft ticket in the workflow system.

The stakes just changed.

If a chatbot is wrong, it produces a bad answer. If an agent is wrong, it can label the wrong Company B as high-risk, notify the wrong person, or modify real business data. It is like giving a very articulate new hire access to internal systems. Suddenly the onboarding guide and business rules matter quite a lot.

An agent therefore needs more than a larger pile of data. It needs to know which records refer to the same thing, which relationships to follow, which rules apply, and who is allowed to see or change what. That is why ontology has returned to the conversation.

Returned is the important word. Ontology was not invented for the agent era. It has a long history in knowledge representation and expert systems. In the 1990s it was formalized as a way to share and reuse knowledge, and it continued to develop through the Semantic Web and data-integration work. Agents now move directly across multiple systems, so an older tool is finding a new context.

This is less a brand-new answer than an old tool being placed back on the workbench.

What can an ontology actually enable?

The first benefit is a shared vocabulary. Once we verify and declare that the ERP's vendor_id, production's partner_code, and a legal name in a contract all refer to Company B, that identity can be reused across questions. Similar names do not magically make two records the same entity; identifier reconciliation or entity matching still has to establish that fact.

The second is relationship traversal. We can move from Product Z to its components, from a component to Company B, and from Company B to contracts and delivery history. Questions that are awkward inside one table become easier to compose across sources.

The third is inference. Suppose the ontology says that batteries are critical components, B-21 is a battery, Company B supplies B-21, and any company supplying a critical component is classified as a critical-component supplier. A reasoner can classify Company B accordingly even if that exact sentence was never stored in the source data.

Those relationships and rules also provide the skeleton of an explanation. The system can record which source facts and which rule led Company B to appear as a candidate. In work where decisions must be justified, that may matter far more than an eloquent answer.

The operational bill arrives with the potential

So far, this sounds excellent. Scattered data connects. AI learns the company's language. The system can even explain its evidence. Draw a few clean arrows on a slide and we are done.

Sure.

An ontology does not clean the source data. If Company B's identifier is mapped incorrectly, the graph will be wrong with impressive consistency. A badly written rule produces a reliably bad conclusion. An LLM can still misread evidence retrieved through the ontology.

More importantly, concepts and relationships do not appear by themselves. Someone has to extract tacit knowledge from domain experts, reconcile conflicting definitions between teams, and map those definitions to source data. When the organization or the process changes, the semantic model must change too. Data quality, permissions, lineage, change management, and monitoring all come along for the ride.

An ontology is therefore neither a hallucination-off switch nor a replacement for RDBs or RAG. It supplies trusted facts and explicit rules that can reduce some errors and make failures easier to trace.

For a simple lookup, use SQL. For answers contained in a few documents, RAG may deliver value sooner. But if several systems represent the same concept differently, questions repeatedly traverse multiple relationships, and the same rules and evidence must be reused, an ontology becomes worth considering.

So the first question should not be, “Should we adopt ontology?”

Does this problem genuinely require an explicit semantic model?

I left the bootcamp seeing much more potential in ontology. I also left wondering who defines all those concepts, who keeps them current, and how we prove that the resulting improvement is worth the cost. Leave those questions out, and the story becomes suspiciously pretty.

My conclusion for Part 1 is modest: an ontology is not a new database that turns AI into an all-purpose expert. It is a semantic layer over existing data that makes an organization's concepts, relationships, and rules explicit, so people, systems, and agents can follow the same meanings. It can be powerful where that shared meaning is genuinely needed—but the cost of agreement and operation is part of the package.
