Understanding DDIM (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models)

Junyoung Park · 2023-04-20 · 12 min

Introduction

Unlike GANs, whose adversarial training does not allow a latent prior to be specified directly, DDPM emerged as a promising new kind of generative model because it avoids mode collapse and can be trained stably.

During training, however, DDPM adds tiny Gaussian noise values to individual pixels over many iterations (T=1000T = 1000T=1000), and sampling is based on the noised input (xtx_txt​) at each step. It therefore cannot escape a Markov process.

A Markov process is a useful way to construct a stable prior p(z)p(z)p(z), but every advantage comes with a cost: DDPM sampling is remarkably slow. In a Markov process, each next state depends on the preceding one in a cascade, which cannot be parallelized on a hardware accelerator such as a GPU. In practice, generating images at the same resolution requires a thousand times more computation than an implicit model such as a GAN. For example, a GAN can generate 50k50k50k images of size 32×3232 \times 3232×32 in under a minute, whereas DDPM takes 20 hours. For more detail on the equations and diffusion itself, see the introductory DDPM post and the post deriving the DDPM equations.

This paper focuses on the excessively slow DDPM sampling process and proposes a new method that retains the same objective as DDPM while enabling a non-Markovian process during sampling. In contrast to DDPM’s fully stochastic sampling, this process is deterministic—conditioned on the original image x0x_0x0​—and creates a one-to-one mapping trajectory like a neural-network decoder. The authors call it a DDIM (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Model).

Overview

The idea behind DDPM was to train the reverse process to follow the posterior q(xt−1∣xt,x0)q(x_{t-1} \vert x_t, x_0)q(xt−1​∣xt​,x0​), which makes the Markovian forward process tractable. DDIM assumes a non-Markovian process and thus enables deterministic sampling. By explicitly stating that the noised input at time ttt originates from x0x_0x0​, as shown below, it escapes the approach that depended only on the preceding state.

In other words, we can ensure that the distribution followed by the original pθ(xt−1∣xt)p_\theta(x_{t-1} \vert x_{t})pθ​(xt−1​∣xt​) is non-Markovian rather than Markovian. The diffusion process can thereby remain implicitly aware, during sampling, of the image x0x_0x0​ that it must recover.

Most importantly, this change does not alter the objective function. There is no need to retrain with a different objective: a pretrained DDPM can be used as-is by applying only the sampling procedure derived in the figure above. A further advantage is that, because deterministic sampling is aware of x0x_0x0​, the reverse process need not traverse all T=1000T = 1000T=1000 steps used by the forward process to create a noised sample. It can instead follow a subsequence Tsub=[τn, τn−1,⋯ , τ0]T_\text{sub} = [\tau_n,~\tau_{n-1}, \cdots,~\tau_0]Tsub​=[τn​, τn−1​,⋯, τ0​] and sample without a large loss of quality.

Finally, because every noised image xt(t<T)x_{t(t < T)}xt(t<T)​ in the sampling process is conditioned on x0x_0x0​, interpolation in the latent space can produce meaningful semantic interpolation.

Image editing with GANs was comparatively straightforward—for example, through GAN inversion—because the mapping from latent zzz through a style mapper FFF and image synthesizer GGG to the implicitly generated x=F(G(z))x = F(G(z))x=F(G(z)) nonlinearly warped the information in the spaces of zzz and xxx. To some degree, interpolating the two latents z1z_1z1​ and z2z_2z2​ that produce x1x_1x1​ and x2x_2x2​ as α⋅z1+(1−α)⋅z2\alpha \cdot z_1 + (1-\alpha) \cdot z_2α⋅z1​+(1−α)⋅z2​ could therefore have an effect in image space analogous to β⋅x1+(1−β)x2\beta \cdot x_1 + (1-\beta)x_2β⋅x1​+(1−β)x2​. Because the mapping is nonlinear, this affine expression is not exact, but it enables the intuitive semantic operation of producing, for instance, a brunette between a blonde woman and a woman with black hair.

Stochasticity prevented such intuitive sampling in a diffusion process. DDIM offers one way to overcome that limitation.

Remarkably, the DDIM equation can be derived directly from this expression. The proof is based on Equation 2.115 in Bishop’s Pattern Recognition—the formula for marginalizing a particular variable from a Gaussian conditional distribution. In brief, suppose we have

Then the distribution of yyy after projecting out xxx is

p(y)=N(y∣Aμ+b, L−1+AΛ−1A⊤)p(y) = \mathcal{N}(y \vert A\mu+b,~\mathrm{L}^{-1}+ A \Lambda^{-1}A^\top)p(y)=N(y∣Aμ+b, L−1+AΛ−1A⊤)

From a non-Markovian posterior to the DDPM loss

Now let us verify why optimizing the qqq defined above yields the same loss as DDPM. Only the qqq term changes relative to the DDPM loss, so we can express the result as follows by replacing the forward process in DDPM’s lower bound with the non-Markovian one.

Sampling with DDIM

Using the predicted ϵθ\epsilon_\thetaϵθ​ in the non-Markovian forward process defined above yields the following sampling equation:

The equation first predicts x0x_0x0​ and then reconstructs xtx_txt​. There is no strict need to predict the sample at t−1t-1t−1; predicting the sample at t−2t-2t−2 is also reasonable. Because it no longer depends only on the preceding state, dependence on a contiguous sequence disappears as well.
