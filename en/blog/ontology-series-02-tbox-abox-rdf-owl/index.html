TBox, ABox, RDF, and OWL: What Do They Actually Do?

Junyoung Park · 2026-09-30 · 14 min

← Part 1: I Went to an AI Bootcamp and Aristotle Showed Up

In the previous post, I described an ontology as a semantic map of how an organization sees its world, written in a form that machines can use.

That sounds manageable. Then you open an ontology tutorial and meet TBox, ABox, RBox, RDF, RDFS, OWL, SHACL, and SPARQL on the same page. Everything is capitalized, and half of it looks like a router model number.

Memorizing the terms alphabetically did not help me much. Instead, this post follows one question from a fictional manufacturer, Company A.

“Find the critical parts and suppliers used by Product Z, then suggest alternative suppliers for the ones with recent delivery delays.”

One sentence from a user, several weeks of work for everyone behind the screen. Business as usual.

Connections Need Meaning Before They Need More Edges

Company A's production team explains the situation like this:

“Product Z uses battery B-21. Company B is the primary supplier, but it delivered late three times last month. Company C could replace it, as long as the certification grade matches.”

A whiteboard fills up quickly.

Product Z ──uses──▶ Battery B-21
Company B ──supplies──▶ Battery B-21
Delivery #D102 ──has supplier──▶ Company B
Delivery #D102 ──has status──▶ Delivery delay
Company C ──can supply──▶ Battery B-21

We already have something that looks like a graph. A few arrows later, however, the uncomfortable questions begin.

  • Is Product Z a product family or one specific model?
  • Is B-21 a part category, a specification, or one physical item in a warehouse?
  • Does “can supply” mean technically certified, commercially contracted, or both?
  • Does one late day count as a delay?
  • Who decides whether a supplier is a valid alternative?

Drawing an edge is easy. Agreeing on what the edge means is the actual work. This is where TBox, ABox, and RBox enter the story.

TBox Defines Terms, ABox Makes Assertions, RBox Describes Relationships

The T in TBox comes from “terminological.” A TBox describes the kinds of things that exist in the modeled domain and the general relationships among those concepts.

A battery is a part.
A critical part is a part.
A supplier is an organization.
A delivery delay is a supply event.

It is the setting guide for this particular world. The goal is not richer lore. The goal is fewer meetings about what “supplier” means.

The A in ABox comes from “assertional.” An ABox contains assertions about particular individuals.

Product Z is a product.
Battery B-21 is a battery.
Company B is a supplier.
Company B supplies Battery B-21.
Delivery #D102 was promised for September 3.

“Assertion” is the important word here. An ABox is not a certificate of truth. It can contain incomplete data, stale data, or confidently imported mistakes. It can describe current events, historical records, or future plans.

In many production systems, the TBox changes carefully while ABox data is refreshed frequently by pipelines. That is a common operating pattern, not the definition of either box.

RBox covers the properties of roles, or relationships. We can declare supplies and isSuppliedBy as inverse properties, define property hierarchies, or state that a relationship is symmetric or transitive. ancestorOf remains meaningful across multiple steps. directPartOf does not: once a step is skipped, it is no longer direct. Adding transitivity because a property name “sounds right” is an efficient way to corrupt meaning without producing a stack trace.


The image labels are in Korean: TBox defines types and general rules, ABox records assertions about individuals, and RBox describes relationship semantics.

These are not necessarily three separate databases. They are three roles within one knowledge model. Some literature also treats RBox statements as part of the TBox.

RDF Writes a Statement in Three Slots

Once the meanings are clear enough, we need a representation that software can process.

RDF, the Resource Description Framework, represents a statement as a triple in subject-predicate-object order.

Company B / supplies / Battery B-21
Product Z / uses / Battery B-21
Delivery #D102 / promised on / 2026-09-03

In other words: what are we talking about, through which property or relationship, and what are we saying about it?

Here is the same example in Turtle, one of several RDF syntaxes.

@prefix ex: <https://example.com/supply/> .
@prefix xsd: <http://www.w3.org/2001/XMLSchema#> .

ex:ProductZ a ex:Product ;
    ex:usesPart ex:BatteryB21 .

ex:SupplierB a ex:Supplier ;
    ex:supplies ex:BatteryB21 .

ex:DeliveryD102 a ex:Delivery ;
    ex:hasSupplier ex:SupplierB ;
    ex:promisedOn "2026-09-03"^^xsd:date .

In Turtle, a is shorthand for rdf:type. An IRI such as ex:ProductZ identifies a resource.

Identifiers do not solve entity resolution by themselves. If three source systems mint three IRIs for the same company, we now have three uniquely identified duplicates. Matching entities and governing identifiers remain real work. Technology has once again declined to clean the room for us.

RDF is not the same thing as XML, either. RDF is a graph data model. RDF/XML is only one serialization, alongside formats such as Turtle and JSON-LD.

From RDFS to OWL: Classification and Reasoning

RDF gives us the basic statement model. RDFS, or RDF Schema, adds vocabulary for classes, properties, and hierarchies.

It can express statements such as “Battery is a subclass of Part,” or describe the domain and range of supplies.

There is an important trap here. RDFS domain and range are not database validation constraints. Suppose an unknown resource X supplies a part and the domain of supplies is Supplier. An RDFS reasoner may conclude that X is a supplier. It does not necessarily reject the record because X was missing an explicit supplier type.

OWL, the Web Ontology Language, can express richer logical semantics: class equivalence and disjointness, inverse properties, cardinality restrictions, and more. These formal statements are called axioms.

An axiom is not a certificate that a claim is universally true. It is a premise adopted by the ontology. A reasoner uses that premise, but people and data processes still have to make sure it reflects reality.

Consider the following definitions and facts.

Battery is a subclass of Critical Part.
A Critical Part Supplier is a supplier that supplies at least one Critical Part.
Battery B-21 is a Battery.
Company B is a Supplier and supplies Battery B-21.

A reasoner can first classify B-21 as a Critical Part, then classify Company B as a Critical Part Supplier. The source database did not need a critical_part_supplier = true column. The result follows from the explicit definitions and facts.


The Korean diagram shows a two-step inference: B-21 becomes a Critical Part, which lets Company B become a Critical Part Supplier.

This is not an LLM making a plausible guess. It is a logical consequence of the stated premises. Of course, bad premises still produce a beautifully consistent bad conclusion. Consistency and truth are not synonyms.

OWL is not the natural home for every business rule. Questions such as “Is this contract valid today?” or “Has the due date passed without a completion record?” involve time, missing operational data, and exceptions. SPARQL, SHACL, a rule engine, or application code may be a better fit. Being expressible somewhere does not mean it belongs there.

OWA: Missing Does Not Automatically Mean False

One of the strangest ideas for newcomers is OWL's Open World Assumption, or OWA.

A typical business application often makes decisions against the records currently stored in its database. If an employee certificate table has no row for a person, the UI may display “No registered certificate.” That is close to a closed-world treatment of the application data.

OWL does not conclude that the certificate does not exist in reality just because this graph does not mention it. The data may be incomplete, or another system may know more. Absence and ignorance are different states.

Cardinality restrictions make this distinction concrete. Saying in OWL that every employee has at least one manager is not the same as a SQL NOT NULL constraint. The ontology may be satisfiable because some manager exists even if that manager has no name in the current graph. Whether a manager value was actually submitted is a validation question.

SHACL: Fine, but Is the Field Filled In?

SHACL, the Shapes Constraint Language, checks whether selected RDF data conforms to required shapes.

Company A might require an approved supplier record to have:

  • exactly one business identifier;
  • at least one part it can supply;
  • a certification expiry value with a date datatype; and
  • an approval status from an allowed list.

A SHACL shape can validate target nodes against those conditions. It does not magically turn the entire universe into a closed world. We still have to choose the data graph, shapes, and targets being validated.

A useful distinction is:

  • OWL asks, “If these facts and axioms hold, what else follows?”
  • SHACL asks, “Does this submitted data have the values and structure we require?”

The comic is in Korean: OWL says “still unknown,” while SHACL points out that the required field is, in fact, still empty.

The world may remain philosophically open. The Save button does not have to be.

SPARQL Describes the Graph Pattern to Find

Now we have data and a semantic model. We need a query language.

SPARQL queries RDF graphs. SQL and SPARQL can both filter and join data, but they target different models. In SPARQL, we describe the graph pattern we want to match.

A query for Product Z's critical parts and their suppliers might look like this.

PREFIX ex: <https://example.com/supply/>

SELECT ?part ?supplier
WHERE {
  ex:ProductZ ex:usesPart ?part .
  ?part a ex:CriticalPart .
  ?supplier ex:supplies ?part .
}

There is one crucial caveat: using SPARQL does not automatically enable OWL reasoning. If B-21 is explicitly typed only as a Battery, this query will see it as a Critical Part only when the store applies the relevant RDFS or OWL entailment, when inferred triples have been materialized, or when the query explicitly follows the required schema paths.

SPARQL is not a strict superset of SQL. Graph queries can feel natural when traversing Product → Part → Supplier → Certification. A warehouse and SQL may be much more mature and efficient for aggregating billions of shipment rows. Different tools have different home games.

The Existing RDB Does Not Need to Disappear

Mention ontologies near a DBA and you can sometimes watch a backup strategy form behind their eyes.

“So we are replacing every relational database with a graph?”

No. Please put the backup disk down.

There are three broad ways to connect an ontology to existing relational data.

First, materialize selected data as RDF in a triple store. This makes graph traversal and reasoning convenient, but the copy must stay synchronized with the source. Schema changes also require mapping changes.

Second, keep the data in place and expose a virtual graph. A system can accept a SPARQL query, use the ontology and mappings to rewrite it into SQL, and execute it against the existing RDB. This family of approaches is called Ontology-Based Data Access, or OBDA.

R2RML is a W3C mapping language for describing how rows and columns in relational logical tables produce RDF subjects, predicates, and objects. R2RML is not itself a reasoner or a SPARQL-to-SQL query engine. An OBDA system uses mappings and ontology information to perform rewriting and execution.

Nor can arbitrary OWL expressions always be translated into SQL. OBDA systems often use deliberately restricted profiles such as OWL 2 QL because they are designed for efficient query rewriting.

Third, use a hybrid. Stable reference entities and important relationships may live in a graph, while high-volume transactions and logs remain in an RDB or warehouse. In practice, this is often the least dramatic option. Not every nail needs the same hammer.

Using Text-to-SQL and Text-to-SPARQL Together

Users generally do not want to write either SQL or SPARQL. They want to ask a question in their own language.

Text-to-SQL, also called NL2SQL, converts a natural-language request into SQL. Text-to-SPARQL, or NL2SPARQL, maps a request to concepts and relationships in an RDF graph. The target languages differ, but both systems face the same hard problem: grounding ambiguous words in the correct schema elements. A query can be syntactically impressive and still mean the wrong thing.

For our original question, a system might:

  • use the graph to find the path from Product Z to critical parts and suppliers;
  • use the warehouse to calculate actual delays and shipment aggregates;
  • search contract documents for substitution clauses; and
  • combine the results with provenance and the rules used.

The Korean diagram shows a parallel hybrid flow: relationships from the graph, large numeric aggregates from the RDB, and contract terms from documents.

These paths do not need to form one long relay race. The planner can query the graph, RDB, and documents in parallel, then merge the results. In a traditional OBDA setup, a Text-to-SPARQL result may be rewritten directly into SQL by the OBDA engine; it does not have to pass through a second Text-to-SQL model. The diagram is one possible architecture, not a mandatory pipeline.

Generated queries also need guardrails: read-only credentials, allowlisted tables and properties, cost limits, user-specific authorization, validation, and audit logs. A write operation should usually require human approval or a separate policy decision.

The fact that an AI understands a Korean or English sentence does not imply that it should be free to query or modify every internal system. There is quite a lot of architecture hiding between those two statements.

The Hard Part Is Still the Definition

Return to the original question.

“Find Product Z's critical parts and suppliers, then suggest alternative suppliers for the ones with delivery delays.”

We can now see the machinery behind it. The TBox defines critical parts and suppliers. The ABox contains assertions about Product Z and Company B. RDF connects the statements. OWL formalizes some semantics, SHACL validates incoming data, SPARQL finds relationships, and the RDB handles large numerical workloads.

One question remains.

Who defines “critical part,” “delivery delay,” and “valid alternative”?

Domain experts carry a large collection of exceptions in their heads. A one-day delay may be harmless when enough safety stock exists. Two suppliers with the same certification may still be non-interchangeable under the contract. Extracting and negotiating that tacit knowledge is one of the difficult parts of ontology design.

Once the definitions are agreed on, an even more practical question arrives.

“How much will this cost?”

The next post looks at Competency Questions and ORSD, then asks what implementation, maintenance, and measurable value actually look like. A proof of concept can make almost anything look elegant—right up until the production button is pressed and the organization chart changes.

Short Version

  • TBox describes general concepts and axioms, ABox contains assertions about individuals, and RBox describes relationship semantics.
  • RDF represents statements as subject-predicate-object triples; RDFS and OWL add vocabulary and formal meaning.
  • OWL reasoning derives consequences from stated premises. It does not certify that the premises are true in the real world.
  • OWA distinguishes “unknown” from “false,” while SHACL checks whether selected data conforms to required shapes.
  • SPARQL does not enable OWL reasoning by itself.
  • R2RML describes relational-to-RDF mappings; an OBDA system uses mappings and an ontology to rewrite and execute queries.
  • Graphs, relational databases, and documents can be queried in parallel rather than forced through one sequential pipeline.
