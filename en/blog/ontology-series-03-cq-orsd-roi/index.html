So, Is an Ontology Worth the Money? A Practical Start with CQs and ORSD

Junyoung Park · 2026-10-01 · 16 min

This is the final post in a three-part introduction to ontology. Part 1 — I Went to an AI Bootcamp and Aristotle Showed Up · Part 2 — TBox, ABox, RDF, and OWL: What Does Any of That Mean?

The companies, products, and numbers in this post are fictional examples.

Everyone was happy while the graph unfolded across the screen.

Click Product Z and its critical components appeared. Click a component and there were its suppliers. Click a supplier and the delivery history and contracts lined up neatly. When we asked, “Which supplier caused last month's production disruption?” the internal agent answered with supporting evidence.

Oh. This actually works.

Then an executive asked:

“So how much would it cost to roll this out across the company?”

The room went quiet for a moment.

I asked a similar question at the bootcamp and received an honest answer: you cannot know the exact cost or impact until you build it and use it in practice.

That is not wrong. You cannot estimate an ontology by counting classes. The scale depends on how tangled the data is, how much knowledge lives only in employees' heads, how many systems need to connect, and how often the rules change. Some problems reveal themselves only after the mapping work begins.

Still, “Let's build it first and find out whether it helps later” is a frightening sentence when you are paying the bill. An honest statement of uncertainty can become a blank check if it has no boundary.

The project may begin with supplier delivery risk. A few meetings later, exchange rates, weather, port strikes, and geopolitical risk have all entered the graph. They are relevant to the supply chain. The logic is fine. The scope is dead.

So rather than spend this post praising what ontology might enable, I want to ask how far that promise deserves our trust. Three questions matter:

Which questions will it answer? How far will we build? What must improve before we continue?

These would be excellent questions before the demo. Naturally, they tend to feel urgent only after everyone has seen the demo.

Tacit knowledge hides inside “that's just how we do it”

Designing an ontology requires domain knowledge. That means more than definitions copied from a manual. It includes the judgments experienced employees make almost automatically when they see a situation.

That knowledge often lives in people's heads and resists a clean explanation. That is why it is called tacit knowledge.

Imagine Company A holds a meeting to define “late delivery.”

“Is every delivery one day past the contractual date late?”

Procurement says yes. Production says weekends and holidays should not count. Logistics says the relevant date is arrival at Company A's distribution center, not departure from the supplier. A domain expert adds that if there is a week of safety stock, a three-day delay may have no effect on production at all.

Hold on. We had one term and now we have four definitions.

It is hard to call any team wrong. Each is looking at the same event through a different operational lens.

Building an ontology does not mean choosing one definition and declaring it universal truth. It means separating contractual delay, receiving delay, production-impacting delay, and risk-triggering delay, then making explicit which definition applies in which context.

Once that is written down, an old argument starts to make sense. People were not disagreeing about one delay. They were using one word for several different delays. The agreement created through this exercise can help the organization even if it never ships a production ontology.

It also reveals where the cost comes from.

Procurement, Production, Logistics, and Data must sit together, find the exceptions, verify that the necessary data exists, and decide who can approve a rule. Saying that calendar coordination may be harder than the technology is not entirely a joke.

CQ: the exam the ontology must pass

It is impossible to extract and model all of a company's tacit knowledge. We need to decide which questions matter first.

A CQ, or Competency Question, is a natural-language question used to check whether an ontology can represent the concepts and relationships required for its purpose. In a live product, the larger system—including connected data and applications—eventually answers it. Methodologically, however, a CQ begins by making the ontology's intended scope and capability concrete.

The first draft often looks like this:

“Show me risky suppliers.”

It looks like a question, but it is not a useful test yet. What counts as risky? Over which period? Risk to what? What evidence should the answer include? Ask a person and they would quite reasonably respond, “Which kind of risk do you mean?”

After a few conversations, the question might become:

“Show me suppliers that missed the contractual delivery date by at least two days during the last 30 days, where the affected component has fewer than five days of safety stock. Include the products at risk, qualified alternative suppliers, and the sources of the delivery, inventory, and certification data used in the decision.”

Now the requirements begin to surface.

We need concepts for suppliers, components, products, deliveries, inventory, and certifications. We need relationships from suppliers to components and from components to products. We need contractual and actual receipt dates, a definition of safety stock, and certification or contract conditions for determining whether an alternative is valid. The result also needs provenance.

A CQ is not a polished list of questions for a slide. It is a tool for working backward to the model and data the system needs.

The diagram is labeled in Korean, but the flow is simple: sharpen the question, then derive the requirements. A good CQ can later become a regression test that asks whether the system still produces the right answer after the model or data changes.

A CQ is not an ROI document by itself. Its original job is to set scope and evaluate whether the ontology is fit for purpose. In practice, though, we can add baselines such as current task time, error rate, and rework cost, turning the CQ into a starting point for an ROI hypothesis.

Suppose an analyst currently downloads delivery history from the ERP, checks inventory in the production system, asks Quality for the certification list, and combines everything in a spreadsheet. It takes three hours, happens ten times per month, and twice a month the work must be repeated because supplier codes did not match.

If an ontology-backed system answers the same question with evidence in three minutes, we have something to compare. If an existing SQL report already returns an accurate answer in five minutes, the case for adding a complex semantic model becomes much weaker. Both are more useful than saying the system “feels smarter.”

ORSD: a fence against sideways growth

Defining CQs does not end the scoping problem. Exchange rates, weather, and port strikes all affect supply-chain risk. If “related” is enough to qualify, the ontology will keep expanding toward the edge of the universe.

This is where an ORSD—Ontology Requirements Specification Document—helps.

ORSD is one of the requirements artifacts proposed by the NeOn methodology for ontology development. It can capture the purpose, scope, users, usage scenarios, expected degree of formality, CQs, and core terminology. It is not a mandatory international template that every project must follow. Think of it as a requirements document to adapt for the organization and project.

For a practical PoC, it can also include data sources, permissions, success criteria, and explicit exclusions. A condensed ORSD for Company A might look like this:

Purpose
- Detect delivery risk for critical components early, with evidence and alternatives.

In scope
- Product S and Z lines at Company A's domestic plant
- Batteries, displays, and application processors; last 12 months of data
- ERP deliveries, production inventory, and quality-certification documents

Not in scope this time
- Overseas plants, exchange rates, weather, or geopolitical-risk forecasting
- Automatic supplier sanctions or contract changes

Success criteria
- The first 15 CQs exceed the agreed targets for answer quality and evidence match.
- Manual effort falls by the target amount, with no exposure of unauthorized data.

The most important section may be Not in scope this time.

Projects rarely expand because someone maliciously destroys the scope. They expand one good idea at a time. Repeat “That is related too; wouldn't it be useful?” ten times and you are running a different project.

An ORSD is not a document for throwing ideas away. It is a fence separating the hypothesis we are testing now from the ideas we may revisit later.

Weather and exchange rates really are relevant to the supply chain. Relevance alone does not put them inside this project.

Build from the top, the bottom, or the middle?

Once the boundary is visible, we still have to choose a modeling direction.

A top-down approach begins with standards and broad concepts such as organizations, people, events, and time. It offers consistency and reuse, but the first useful answer may arrive late. It is entirely possible to spend the budget defining an enterprise standard before answering the first CQ.

A bottom-up approach starts with the vocabulary that already exists in ERP tables, spreadsheet columns, and contracts. It connects to real data quickly, but it can reproduce the duplication and silos of existing systems in a prettier graph. Moving garbage into a graph does not turn it into treasure.

A middle-out approach can be a practical compromise: begin with the intermediate concepts needed by the core CQs, then expand upward and downward. Company A might start with products, critical components, suppliers, deliveries, inventory, and certifications. Common organization and contract concepts—or detailed component specifications—can be added when a question requires them.

The goal is not a perfect model on the first attempt. Build a small core, test it against actual data and CQs, and revise it. Of course, every technology project claims it will “start small.” Put numbers around small, or it tends to become a mood rather than a boundary.

The real cost is outside the graph database price sheet

When people ask what an ontology costs, licenses and servers are easy to picture. They do cost money. The larger bill, however, may come from people and change.

The initial build requires domain interviews, terminology agreements, identifier policies, source-data cleanup, mappings to RDBs and documents, axioms and validation rules, permissions and lineage, and reference answers for each CQ. Agreeing on the meaning and facts that enter the graph may take longer than drawing the graph.

What happens after launch matters even more.

The organization restructures. Supplier codes and database columns change. The ERP is replaced. New products and component categories appear. Certification rules and document formats move on. Each time, someone must determine which parts of the TBox, ABox generation pipeline, mappings, SHACL shapes, access policies, and CQ tests are affected.

That is the ongoing operating cost. In addition to inference, storage, and LLM usage, the system needs people who can update models and mappings, domain owners who approve changes, regression tests, and incident response.

An ontology is not a diagram that gets delivered once and framed on a wall. It models reality, so it must change when reality changes. In practice, the project creates a new operational responsibility.

Who approves a terminology change? Does Procurement or Production own a supply-risk rule? Who reviews relationships extracted automatically? Without an organization that can answer those questions, the ontology slowly goes stale. A stale semantic model may be more dangerous than no model at all, because users may still trust it as the official definition.

Some problems simply do not need an ontology

If the question is “Show last month's revenue by product,” a well-designed SQL view or data mart may be faster, cheaper, and more accurate. If the tables are stable and the questions are predictable, Text-to-SQL may be sufficient.

If the job is to find the vacation allowance in a policy document, start by making search and RAG good. If customer IDs are broken across systems and every source uses a different date format, data quality and master data management may need to come before ontology.

The value of an ontology grows when the same concept repeatedly appears under different names across systems, questions traverse several relationships, and rules and evidence must be reused by multiple services. That is especially true when decisions require a traceable path and someone is prepared to own the semantic model.

The important question is not whether the problem is complex.

Does a significant part of the complexity come from inconsistent meaning and relationships?

Adding more meetings will not make a slow server faster. Adding more servers will not resolve a conflict in meaning.

Run a comparison, not just a PoC demo

If the only way to know is to try it, then try it on a small scale before building it everywhere. But a feature demo—one where the graph appears and a natural-language answer comes back—is not enough. The real question is whether this approach performs better than the alternatives.

Company A could limit the trial to two production lines, three types of critical components, three data sources, and 15 priority CQs. Keep the agent read-only and operate it for 8–12 weeks.

Then answer the same questions in three ways:

  1. The current manual workflow or existing reports
  2. An LLM with RAG or Text-to-SQL
  3. An LLM with an ontology, knowledge graph, and rules

Do not decide the winner in advance. Use the same CQs, the same snapshot of the data, and the same reference answers and evidence. Compare answer quality, elapsed time, evidence agreement, number of manual rechecks, operational person-hours, change cost, and permission violations.

One distinction is particularly important: separate the data cleanup that was necessary anyway from the additional cost created by choosing ontology.

Deduplicating supplier codes in the ERP may have been necessary with or without an ontology. Operating OWL axioms, a graph store, SPARQL endpoints, and mappings is an incremental cost of this architecture. It is unfair to blame the ontology for every cleanup cost. It is equally unfair to claim every benefit of cleaner data as an ontology win.

Define exit criteria alongside success criteria

A project with only success criteria can keep running toward success indefinitely. The awkward question is how long it gets to run.

Before starting, define reasons to stop. For example:

  • Core CQs do not improve meaningfully over the existing RAG or Text-to-SQL approach.
  • Maintaining the model and mappings consumes more time than the workflow saves.
  • Teams cannot reach agreement on the core concepts.
  • Every new data source requires rebuilding most of the model.
  • Permission and lineage requirements remain unmet, or users do not trust the output.

Expansion should require the opposite kind of evidence. Core CQs should become materially more accurate and better supported. Concepts and mappings created once should be reusable in another use case. Regression tests should work after data changes. The approach should still look better than its alternatives after including one to three years of operating cost.

Setting numbers is difficult. Not setting them leaves us with “It keeps getting better,” which is useful for extending a project and much less useful for deciding whether the investment makes sense.

Demo accuracy is a starting point, not the receipt for operating cost. The organization, data, and permissions will not sit still; keeping the model alive begins after the applause.

My conclusion is still neither yes nor no

The bootcamp made the potential of ontology easy to see.

As agents move through several systems and take actions, an organization's shared meanings and relationships become more important. Ontology is especially attractive for relational questions that document similarity cannot answer well, decisions that require evidence, and semantic integration across systems.

I am still skeptical too.

An ontology is not a flawless copy of reality. It is a model that people agree on for a particular purpose. Bad data, bad agreements, and stale rules can enter it. Before looking closely at the data and tacit knowledge, it is hard to estimate the build. Before attaching it to real work, it is hard to know the impact. Maintenance cost rarely looks impressive on a demo screen.

“We will know after we build and use it” can be an honest acknowledgment of that uncertainty.

It must not become a blank check.

My conclusion is neither “Every company needs an ontology” nor “It is expensive, so forget it.”

Choose a small set of questions and test, under the same conditions, whether ontology actually earns its keep against SQL and RAG.

Use CQs to write the exam and an ORSD to build the fence. Compare the alternatives fairly, including maintenance time. Expand if it clears the threshold. Stop if it does not.

A failed experiment can still teach the organization a great deal about its data and business definitions.

Just decide where to stop before you begin.

For now, that is the most realistic way I know to look at ontology.

The short version

  • Ontology design extracts tacit domain knowledge into concepts, relationships, and rules that an organization can agree on.
  • A CQ's original role is to express an ontology's required scope and competency as questions. Add operational baselines and it can also support an ROI hypothesis.
  • ORSD is a requirements artifact from the NeOn methodology, not a universally mandatory standard. It is useful for defining purpose, inclusions, and exclusions.
  • Cost comes not only from initial modeling and data integration, but also from ongoing approvals, mappings, permissions, regression tests, and maintenance.
  • A PoC should compare alternatives under the same conditions and define both success and exit criteria.
